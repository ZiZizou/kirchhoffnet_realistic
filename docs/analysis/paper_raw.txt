HR-KNet: A hardware-realistic analog accelerator
and design framework
Abstract—Data-center AI systems increasingly depend on highspeed wireline links, making transceiver bandwidth, adaptation,
and energy efficiency central to AI infrastructure. This paper proposes Hardware-Realistic KNet (HR-KNet) for analog control of
high-performance wireline transceivers, illustrating the reciprocal
relationship of AI for chips and chips for AI. HR-KNet computes
directly on voltages through Kirchhoff-current-law-governed ODE
dynamics, but prior formulations leave connectivity, device limits,
and circuit interfaces idealized. We address these gaps with a
sparse Watts-Strogatz fabric, bounded differential-OTA edges with
shunts and rail-compliance protection, explicit sparse input and
accumulator readout paths, and a Bayesian optimization framework that searches topology, solver, and device parameters while
distilling a digital teacher. We evaluate the resulting controller on
programmable-CTLE inverse design for 56–112 Gb/s PAM-4 links.
At matched parameter budgets, HR-KNet improves standard
regression benchmarks. On CTLE, it reduces Cadence-verified
failure rate from 51.0% for a digital mixture-of-experts (MoE)
baseline to 40.5%, while reducing surrogate failure. These results
show how a bounded analog neural fabric can support learned
transceiver tuning without a data converter in the modeled control
path.

Data-center AI workloads depend on moving increasingly
large volumes of data between compute, memory, and networking devices. In this setting, the wireline transceiver is part of the
compute system: its equalization, adaptation, and control loops
determine how quickly and efficiently data can cross the link.
High-speed wireline SerDes is therefore our motivating vehicle.
At 56–112 Gb/s PAM-4, ADC-plus-DSP receivers are common,
but the converters, clocking, and datapath impose substantial
area and power costs before any neural equalizer is added [1]–
[3] (Fig. 1).
This bottleneck exposes a mismatch between the task and the
usual solution. Programmable-CTLE tuning is naturally a lowdimensional analog control problem—measured specifications
enter, and bias codes and trims leave—yet digital multilayer
perceptrons (MLPs) bring dense matrix multiplications, unbounded activations, and all-to-all connectivity. On an analog
chip, those assumptions cost power, area, and routability; onchip data converters add a further tax for moving between
voltage-domain signals and digital adaptation (Fig. 1). An
analog fabric that computes directly on voltages can therefore
close the control loop without placing a converter in the critical
path.

KirchhoffNet (KNet) is an analog-inspired Neural ODE:
a sparse graph of voltage nodes joined by programmable
OTA-like edge devices, where fixed-step integration of the
KCL equations is the computation—no dense digital matrix
multiplies in the core. A forward pass has three phases—
write (u to V (0)), evolve (integrate through graph stages with
learned interstage transfers), read (affine readout to logits,
i.e. pre-activation outputs)—trained by direct backpropagation
through the unrolled integrator (no adjoint [12]). Fig. 3 shows
a four-stage instantiation with stage-varying edge parameters
and continuous Vout accumulation.

II. K IRCHHOFF N ET BACKGROUND AND P ROPOSED
HR-KN ET
The methodology follows the signal path of the intended
transceiver controller. We first define the voltage-state dynamics
and its numerical integrator, then describe the sparse analog fabric—edge devices, routable topology, and input/output
interfaces—as one unit. We next cover the frozen-current
stabilization used across those families. Finally, we introduce
the optional mechanisms used by the CTLE search—inputconditioned transconductance and VCA gating—before describing how the resulting model is trained and evaluated.

HR-KNet retains the voltage-state interpretation of the original model but changes the assumptions that prevent direct
hardware use. Its state evolution is bounded, its graph is sparse,
and its input and output paths are explicit circuit structures.
The following subsections introduce these features in the same
order as a physical signal traverses the fabric: node dynamics,
then the sparse analog fabric (edge devices, topology, and I/O
interfaces with the interstage transfer), followed by frozencurrent stabilization.

F. Optional input-conditioned gain: VCA and GLN rails
Both auxiliary mechanisms below follow one pattern: a
shared input projection, a per-edge tap, and a monotonic gain
that equals 1.0 at initialization and multiplies a single edge
parameter. Each evaluates once per sample (at stage entry) and
folds into the frozen current, so neither adds per-substep analog
dynamics; shunts are never gated. They are strictly optional:
both stay OFF for the synthetic benchmarks and switch on only
for the 7-target CTLE task. Together they form dual trims of
the two OTA degrees of freedom—VCA scales edge currents
via Isat , GLN trims edge transconductances via gm .

The circuit fabric above supplies a circuit-aware compute
primitive; this section connects it to the transceiver-tuning
problem. The key design choice is to separate supervision from
deployment: a searched digital teacher supplies stable target
settings during development, while the deployed HR-KNet
student must map measured link specifications to bounded
device controls without a data converter in the modeled forward path. Demonstrating MLP-to-HR-KNet distillation in this
setting establishes a reusable training path: future analog tasks
can reuse strong digital teachers instead of training the bounded
fabric from scratch.

a single-head plain MLP mapping 4 specifications (power,
jitter, eye height, eye width) to 7 physical device parameters

Rs , VDD ; see Fig. 5 for where each lands on the CTLE). A
mixture-of-experts (MoE) MLP (also optimized via Bayesian
Optimization over the same number of trials as HR-KNet) is
kept alongside purely as a digital baseline (Table II), not as a
teacher.

Failure follows one fixed forward rule: a design fails when

uses those metrics only to rank configurations, not to train them.
The student’s ability to match its MLP teacher on the surrogate
metrics confirms that the bounded analog fabric absorbs digital
knowledge, opening further applications where a digital model
already exists but analog deployment is preferred for power,
area, or converter-less operation.

We evaluate the complete framework in two stages. First,
matched-budget experiments on standard regression tasks test
whether the hardware-aware HR-KNet dynamics retain useful
modeling capacity. Second, the programmable-CTLE study
tests the intended system-level outcome: whether the distilled
analog controller can produce valid transceiver settings and
open the eye in a held-out Cadence circuit simulation.

A. Synthetic Regression Benchmarks
Synthetic benchmarks use direct regression: each candidate
trains 400 fixed epochs with Huber loss on raw targets (no
teacher). Optimization ran under a ≈ 7,000-parameter cap on
Friedman1 and Friedman2 (≈ 10,000 for Franke’s function),
with 50 trials per benchmark. Shared-sense readout and GLN
rails stay OFF here. No quantity is tied to SI units or quantized
to feasible device levels yet—Ceff , tspan , gm , conductances, and
Isat are all continuous—identically for the MLP baselines, so
gaps reflect architecture. The cap binds as an upper bound only:
the Friedman-2 HR-KNet winner uses just 1,069 parameters yet
still beats the 6,763-parameter MLP (optimizing the time span
of operation and VCA parameter). Sparsity pays: the smallworld fabric matches MLP expressivity at roughly one-sixth
the parameters. The strongest trees trail both: LightGBM on
Friedman-1 (RMSE 0.5395 vs. HR-KNet 0.1406) and Franke
(0.0059 vs. HR-KNet 0.0018), with even the best tree baseline
on Friedman-2 at RMSE 8.9546 vs. HR-KNet 1.2718 (Table
I). Budget-matched trees trail by wide margins, confirming
these benchmarks reward the smooth nonlinearities both neural
fabrics provide. Fig. 7(b)–(d) shows the best-found trajectories:
despite searching a higher-dimensional space (topology, solver,
and device bounds for HR-KNet vs. depth, learning rate, weight
decay, batch size, and activation for the MLP). The extra
search dimensions (topology, solver, device bounds) thus pay
for themselves rather than diluting the search.

V. C ONCLUSION
We presented HR-KNet, a hardware-realistic KirchhoffNet
redesign with a routing-aware Watts–Strogatz analog fabric
(k=4, p=0.2), bounded differential OTAs, soft-rail clamping,
and sparse boundary fan-out. Joint topology-horizon optimization via a frozen digital teacher enables HR-KNet to match or
beat MLPs and tree baselines. On a 4→7 CTLE inverse-design
task, it reduces Cadence-verified failure rates from 51.0% to
40.5% while matching eye metrics at fewer parameters. These
results establish a circuit-aware analog-control architecture and
a supervise-then-distill framework; layout, noise, and PVT
validation remain necessary before hardware implementation.
